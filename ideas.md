# Close Knit — Feature Ideas

A brainstorm of what a **family social app** should be, deliberately zoomed out past
the Great Deeds story engine.

> **The reframe.** Great Deeds is a *memory archive*. Archives are visited; they are not
> lived in. A family app that only stores history will be opened on holidays and
> forgotten in between. Everything below aims at a harder target: **be useful on a
> boring Tuesday.** Daily utility earns the right to hold the family's history —
> because the app people actually open is the app they'll trust with their memories.
>
> Ideas are grouped by the *human need* they serve, not by feature type.
> Each is marked with a rough call: **P1** = makes the app worth opening weekly ·
> **P2** = strong follow-on · **P3** = later / speculative.

---

## 1. Staying in touch (the daily reason to open)

The single biggest gap today. There is no way to just... talk.

- **Family chat / group threads** — P1. Table stakes. One general thread plus ad-hoc
  ones ("Mum's birthday planning", "the cousins"). This is the feature that replaces
  the WhatsApp group, and until it exists the WhatsApp group is your real competitor.
- **Voice notes** — P1. Grandparents who won't type will happily talk. Also doubles as
  archive material: a voice note *is* a primary source. Transcribe it and it's searchable.
- **Video rooms / "the kitchen table"** — P2. A persistent, always-there room anyone can
  drop into on Sunday. Not scheduled calls — an open door.
- **Photo dumps with no ceremony** — P1. A "just share it" path that is *not* the
  4-step Deed wizard. The wizard is right for legacy stories and wrong for "the dog
  did something stupid". **Two speeds of posting is the core insight**: quick share
  today, curated story forever.
- **Reactions + threaded replies on everything** — P1. Already built for deeds; extend
  to photos, chat and events.
- **Read receipts, softly** — P3. "Grandad saw this" is genuinely reassuring for a
  family. Show it warmly, never as surveillance or pressure.
- **"Thinking of you" nudge** — P2. One tap sends a warm ping. Zero composition cost,
  which matters enormously for the least tech-confident members.

## 2. Knowing what's going on (coordination)

Families run on logistics. Logistics is what drives *daily* opens.

- **Shared family calendar** — P1. Already planned. Auto-fills birthdays and
  anniversaries from the tree; members add reunions, graduations, hospital visits.
- **Birthday & anniversary radar** — P1. "Nana turns 80 in 3 weeks — 4 people haven't
  said anything yet." Prompts action, not just awareness.
- **Event planning with RSVPs** — P2. Who's coming, who's driving, who's bringing what.
  A reunion is a logistics problem long before it's a memory.
- **Potluck / assignment lists** — P2. Claimable items. Ends the "we have five trifles" call.
- **Shared shopping & to-do lists** — P2. Mundane, high-frequency, sticky.
- **Travel & arrival board** — P3. Who lands when, who needs collecting. Gold at Christmas.
- **Location sharing, opt-in and temporary** — P3. Only for events ("we're 10 min away").
  Permanent family tracking is a different, creepier product — don't drift into it.

## 3. Caring for each other (the underserved need)

Almost nobody builds this well, and it's where families feel real strain.
**This is the most defensible territory in the whole list** — it's the reason a family
would pay, and the reason they'd never switch.

- **Care coordination for an elderly or ill relative** — P1 (huge). Who's visiting when,
  what the doctor said, which prescriptions were collected. Right now this lives in
  frantic sibling texts and someone always drops the ball.
- **Medical history vault** — P2. Blood types, allergies, conditions, "Dad's cardiologist
  is Dr Amara". Genuinely life-saving in an emergency, and a natural extension of a
  family tree that already knows who is related to whom.
- **Emergency info card** — P2. One screen, offline-available: contacts, allergies,
  insurance, who holds power of attorney.
- **Meal trains / help rotas** — P2. New baby, surgery, bereavement. Signup sheets for
  practical help.
- **Check-in pings for someone living alone** — P3. Gentle daily "all fine" tap that
  quietly alerts the family if it stops.
- **Bereavement mode** — P2. When a member dies, the app should behave with grace:
  freeze their profile respectfully, invite memories, never send "wish them a happy
  birthday" notifications again. **Getting this wrong is unforgivable** — an automated
  birthday reminder for someone who died last month is the kind of wound that makes a
  family delete an app forever and tell everyone why.

## 4. Money and the boring grown-up stuff

Real families deal with money together. Handle with care — money is how families fight.

- **Shared expense splitting** — P2. Reunion costs, group gifts, Mum's care bills.
  Splitwise-for-family, inside the circle that already exists.
- **Group gifting pots** — P2. Everyone chips in for one big present. Natural, warm,
  and a plausible transaction-fee revenue line.
- **Document vault** — P2. Wills, deeds, insurance, passports, birth certificates.
  "Where is the will?" is a real and awful question. Encrypted, admin-controlled.
- **Legacy contact & digital inheritance** — P1 for trust (already in strategy).
  Who inherits control of this archive.
- **Allowance / chore tracking for kids** — P3. Pulls in the youngest members, who are
  otherwise pure dead weight in a heritage app.

## 5. Being a family across distance and generations

- **Interactive family tree** — P1. Already the backbone.
- **Relationship explainer** — P2. "How is Tomas related to me?" → *"He's your first
  cousin once removed."* Delightful, genuinely useful at reunions, and something only
  an app that owns the tree can do.
- **Family map** — P2. Where everyone lives now, and where the family came *from*.
  Migration over generations is a moving thing to see drawn.
- **"Who is this?" photo identification** — P2. Post an old photo, let elders tag the
  faces before that knowledge is gone. **This is a race against time** and the most
  urgent thing in the whole app.
- **Recipe box** — P1 (surprisingly). Family recipes are the most-requested heirloom
  there is. Photo of the handwritten card + typed version + "Grandma made this every
  Easter". High emotional value, trivial to build.
- **Family stories on tap for kids** — P3. A read-aloud mode: bedtime stories about
  their own great-grandparents.
- **Language & heritage corner** — P3. Phrases, sayings, jokes that only this family
  uses. Every family has a private dialect; nobody records it.
- **Pets** — P3. They're family. Let them have profiles. People will love this far more
  than it deserves.

## 6. Celebrating and belonging

- **Milestone recognition** — P1. New job, exam passed, first house, sobriety
  anniversary, quitting smoking. Not just births and weddings — the *modern* milestones.
- **Applaud / Inspire / Cherish / Love** — P1. Built. Appreciation, not likes.
- **Family "yearbook" auto-compiled** — P2. Each December, the app assembles the year:
  photos, milestones, new arrivals. Ties straight into the Heirloom PDF and print revenue.
- **Anniversary resurfacing ("On This Day")** — P1. Planned. The cheapest engagement
  engine that exists and it *fits* this product honestly.
- **Family awards / silly superlatives** — P3. Voted annually. "Most likely to be late."
  Families love in-jokes; formalise them.
- **Traditions tracker** — P2. Log the recurring ones so they survive a generation
  change. Traditions die when the one person who organised them stops.

## 7. Keeping it safe (the promise)

Privacy isn't a feature here, it's the foundation — but there are gaps.

- **Invitation-only circles, no discovery** — P1. Built into the concept.
- **Granular visibility** — P2. Some things are for adults only (health, money, wills).
  Some are for one branch. **A single flat circle will eventually fail a real family** —
  divorce, estrangement, a relative nobody wants seeing the kids' photos.
- **Child safety controls** — P1. Kids' photos are the most sensitive content here.
  Parents control what's visible and downloadable, and screenshot-shy defaults.
- **Handling estrangement and divorce** — P2. Blended families, ex-spouses who are still
  a parent, relatives who must not be in the same space. **Most family-tree software
  models an idealised family and quietly insults everyone else.** Handling messiness
  gracefully is a serious competitive advantage.
- **Private admin-led moderation** — P1. Built. No public report buttons, no drama.
- **Full data export, always** — P1. The no-lock-in promise (planned).

## 8. Making it effortless (AI, used with restraint)

AI should reduce *work*, never manufacture memories. **Fabricated family history is
worse than no family history** — the archive's whole value is that it's true.

- **Story assistant (draft only)** — P2. Bullet points → warm first draft, always edited
  and approved by a human before it exists.
- **Voice-note transcription** — P1. The single highest-value AI feature here: it turns
  the way elders naturally communicate into searchable text.
- **Handwriting OCR** — P2. Letters, recipe cards, diaries → searchable.
- **Photo restoration & colourisation** — P2. Huge emotional payoff, clear premium hook.
- **Face grouping for tagging** — P2. Tag once, get suggestions. Must stay in-circle only,
  never a cloud face database.
- **Conversational search** — P3. "Photos of Mum's 50th."
- **Auto-organising incoming photos** — P2. Bulk uploads sorted by person, date and event.
- **Deliberately NOT doing:** AI-animated portraits of the dead (opt-in at most, and
  never a default or a notification), AI-invented stories, AI-generated "memories" of
  events that didn't happen.

## 9. The physical world

- **Heirloom book export → print-on-demand** — P1 for revenue. Planned. The killer feature.
- **Photo prints & framed trees** — P2.
- **Postcard sending** — P3. Grandchild's drawing → real card in Nana's letterbox.
  Bridges the generation that isn't on phones at all.
- **QR "story stones" / plaque codes** — P3. A code on a grave, a family home, an
  heirloom object that opens its story. Odd and lovely.
- **Annual printed calendar** — P2. Auto-built from birthdays and photos. Sells itself
  every November.

## What I'd actually build next

Ranked by *earning the daily open*, since that's the real risk to this product:

1. **Chat + voice notes + quick photo share.** Without these the app is a museum, and
   the family WhatsApp group wins by default. Two posting speeds: quick share vs. curated Deed.
2. **Shared calendar + birthday radar.** The utility loop that creates habit.
3. **Recipe box.** Cheapest emotional win available; disproportionately loved.
4. **"Who is this?" photo tagging.** Time-critical — the elders who can answer are
   the ones we're racing.
5. **Care coordination.** The most defensible, least-served, most-likely-to-be-paid-for
   territory in the entire space.

Then: granular visibility and estrangement handling, before the family grows large
enough for a flat circle to cause a real incident.

## The strategic read

- **The competitor is not Ancestry or MyHeritage. It's the family WhatsApp group** —
  and it wins on daily habit while losing badly on permanence, structure and dignity.
  Beat it on *keeping* things, match it on *talking*.
- **Heritage apps are bought by one person and abandoned by the group.** Utility for
  the whole family is what makes an archive survive its founder.
- **The willing payer is the Family Champion, usually 45–65,** who is simultaneously
  organising a reunion, worrying about a parent's health, and realising nobody wrote
  Grandma's stories down. Care coordination, logistics and preservation are the *same
  job* to that person. Serving all three is the product.
- **Sequencing matters:** daily utility first, memory permanence second, monetised
  heirlooms third. Build it the other way round and there's nobody left using it to sell to.
